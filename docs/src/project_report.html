Abstract

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is the most common blood cancer in children, and picking out the right subtype early from a peripheral blood smear gives clinicians a head start on treatment. The first read still comes down to a hematologist looking at a stained smear under a microscope, which is slow, costly and sensitive to how experienced the reader is. This project builds an automated system that classifies peripheral blood smear images into four classes, Benign, early Pre-B, Pre-B and Pro-B, and reports how confident it is in each decision.

The system is a hybrid ensemble of three pretrained convolutional neural network backbones, MobileNetV2, EfficientNetB0 and DenseNet121, each fine-tuned on the Blood Cells Cancer (ALL) dataset (3,242 images, four classes) with a two-phase transfer-learning schedule. Their softmax outputs are combined in two ways, weighted soft voting and a stacked logistic-regression meta-classifier. On a held-out test split the stacked ensemble reached 98.97% accuracy and a 0.9883 macro-F1, level with the strongest single backbone.

The part we add on top is an uncertainty layer. Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout runs 30 stochastic forward passes per image, and the spread across those passes becomes two numbers, predictive entropy and mutual information, that tell apart predictions the model is sure of from the ones it is not. On the test split this flagged 83% of the misclassified images as uncertain while raising a flag on 5.6% of the correct ones. Grad-CAM overlays confirm that the network attends to nuclear morphology, the same feature a hematologist examines. The whole pipeline runs as a FastAPI web application that reports an uncertainty flag with every prediction.

Keywords: Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, blood cancer, ensemble learning, transfer learning, MC Dropout, uncertainty quantification, Grad-CAM, MobileNetV2, EfficientNetB0, DenseNet121.

Chapter 1: Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation

Blood cancer, or hematological malignancy, happens when the bone marrow stops making blood cells the way it should. Among children it is Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) that shows up most often, accounting for roughly 25 percent of pediatric cancer diagnoses worldwide [1]. In ALL the marrow churns out immature lymphocytes, called lymphoblasts, that crowd out healthy cells and leak into the bloodstream. The subtypes do not behave the same way or respond to the same drugs, so getting the subtype right at the first examination is what shapes the rest of the care.

In practice that first read still comes down to a hematologist looking at a stained blood smear under a microscope, with flow cytometry and molecular tests coming later. It is slow, it is expensive, and the microscope step in particular leans heavily on how experienced the person at the eyepiece is [2]. Where experienced pathologists are in short supply, two readers can disagree on the same slide.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have done well on medical images over the past decade [3], and transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained networks is a practical way to start when labelled data is thin. Letting several models vote together, rather than trusting one, tends to smooth out variance and recover mistakes a single network would make on its own. The motivation for this project was therefore not merely to classify cells accurately, but to build a system that also knows when it is unsure and says so, which is what clinical use demands.

Blood smear image  →  Three CNN backbones  →  Ensemble vote  →  MC Dropout confidence  →  Prediction + uncertainty flag (+ Grad-CAM)
Figure 1.1: High-level block diagram of the proposed system: feature extraction by three backbones, an ensemble vote, an uncertainty estimate and a visual explanation.

1.2 Problem Statement

The project addresses the following problems in automated ALL detection:

  1. Observer dependence: manual microscopy is slow and varies from one reader to another, and the correct subtype is hard to call when two subtypes look alike.
  2. Overconfident models: most existing systems hand back only a softmax score, which says nothing about how stable the prediction is. A network can report 0.92 confidence for a cell sitting right on a decision boundary.
  3. No way to refer hard cases: without an uncertainty estimate there is no principled way to separate the predictions a clinician can trust from the ones that should go to a specialist.
  4. Opacity: a prediction with no visual explanation is difficult for a clinician to trust or to check against what they see under the microscope.

1.3 Scope and Applications

The system classifies pre-cropped single-cell peripheral blood smear images into four classes and returns, for each image, the predicted class, a confidence score, the per-backbone votes, a Grad-CAM overlay and an uncertainty flag. It is intended as a screening-support tool that gives a fast second opinion and routes uncertain cases to a human specialist. It is a research and educational prototype and is explicitly not intended for direct clinical diagnosis. Possible applications include triage support in laboratories with few pathologists, a teaching aid for trainees, and a reusable, reproducible baseline for further research on uncertainty-aware medical image classification.

1.4 Contributions of the Project

  1. A reproducible hybrid ensemble framework on a public dataset, with full source code.
  2. A side-by-side comparison of weighted soft voting and stacked meta-classification.
  3. MC Dropout uncertainty quantification with predictive entropy and mutual information (the primary novelty).
  4. Grad-CAM integration for visual interpretability of every prediction.
  5. A deployable FastAPI web application for clinical screening support.

Chapter 2: Literature Survey

2.1 Existing System

Shafique and Tehsin [7] trained AlexNet alongside a custom CNN on the ALL-IDB dataset and still cleared 95 percent accuracy, a sign that transfer learning holds up even on a small dataset. Mondal et al. [8] took VGG16, ResNet50 and InceptionV3, compared them on blood cell images, and then stacked them into ensembles; the ensembles beat every single model, and soft voting edged out hard voting. Acevedo et al. [9] published a carefully annotated set of peripheral blood cell images and ran several pretrained architectures over it, finding that DenseNet and EfficientNet variants struck the best balance between accuracy and inference cost. Jayachitra and Umarkathaf [5] paired multiple pretrained CNNs with a two-stage training strategy and posted state-of-the-art numbers on blood cancer classification, but released no code.

On the interpretability and uncertainty side, Grad-CAM, introduced by Selvaraju et al. [6], reads the gradients flowing into the last convolutional layer and turns them into a heatmap over the regions that mattered; it is now a go-to tool for explaining CNN decisions in medical imaging [10]. Gal and Ghahramani [16] proved that leaving dropout on at inference approximates Bayesian inference, and Leibig et al. [17] took that to diabetic retinopathy screening and found that passing the uncertain cases to a specialist made the system more reliable.

2.2 Limitations of the Existing System

  • Most systems report a single softmax score, which is a poor stand-in for confidence and cannot flag a borderline prediction.
  • Few provide any visual explanation, so a clinician cannot see what the model looked at.
  • State-of-the-art results are often reported without released code, so they are hard to reproduce or build on.
  • Uncertainty estimation, which is standard in other high-stakes medical imaging tasks, is still absent from most ALL detection work.

2.3 Proposed System

We rebuild the hybrid ensemble idea from [5] with three named backbones and add two things that earlier work left out: MC Dropout uncertainty quantification and Grad-CAM interpretability. Every prediction carries a confidence flag, and low-confidence cases are referred to a human specialist rather than acted on directly. The full pipeline and its web interface are released as open source so the results can be reproduced.

2.4 Objectives

  • Fine-tune three ImageNet-pretrained backbones on the four-class ALL dataset using a two-phase transfer-learning schedule.
  • Combine them through weighted soft voting and a stacked meta-classifier, and compare the two.
  • Add MC Dropout to estimate predictive entropy and mutual information for each prediction.
  • Map the uncertainty to a three-level confidence flag and refer low-confidence cases.
  • Generate Grad-CAM overlays and deploy the system as a web application.

Chapter 3: Proposed System

3.1 Architecture, Algorithms and Methods

The system is organised as a web application on top of a classification pipeline. A plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript front-end talks to a FastAPI backend over HTTP; the backend runs the pipeline stages, each implemented as a separate module in the src/ package, and loads the trained models from disk. Figure 3.1 shows the components and how they connect.

Figure 3.1: Overall architecture of the proposed hybrid ensemble system. Three pretrained CNN backbones are fine-tuned independently, combined via weighted voting or stacked meta-classification, and augmented with Grad-CAM visualisation and MC Dropout uncertainty estimation.

3.1.1 Dataset

Our data is the Blood Cells Cancer (ALL) dataset that Aria et al. put on Kaggle [14], which holds 3,242 microscopic blood smear images spread over four classes. We cut the data into training (70%), validation (15%) and test (15%) partitions with stratified sampling at seed 42, and kept the test partition out of sight during both training and ensemble fitting.

Table 3.1: Dataset class distribution.
ClassDescriptionImages
BenignNormal B-lymphoid precursors~810
[Malignant] early Pre-BALL subtype I~810
[Malignant] Pre-BALL subtype II~810
[Malignant] Pro-BALL subtype III~810

3.1.2 Preprocessing and Augmentation

Every image is resized to 224×224 pixels and normalised through the preprocessing function specific to each backbone. During training we apply horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation (±20°), random zoom (±10%) and brightness/contrast changes (±10%). No augmentation is applied during validation or testing.

3.1.3 CNN Backbone Architectures

We picked three pretrained architectures built differently enough that each tends to latch onto a different aspect of cell morphology. MobileNetV2 [11] leans on inverted residual blocks with depthwise separable convolutions (3.4M parameters). EfficientNetB0 [12] uses compound scaling to trade off width, depth and resolution (5.3M). DenseNet121 [13] wires every layer to every other one inside dense blocks, which encourages feature reuse (8.0M). For all three we removed the original classification head and attached Global Average Pooling, a Dropout layer (rate 0.3) and a Dense layer with four units and softmax activation.

3.1.4 Two-Phase Transfer Learning

In Phase 1 the entire base network is frozen and only the new head is trained (Adam, learning rate 1×10−3, 5 epochs). In Phase 2 the top 30% of base layers are unfrozen and fine-tuned at a low learning rate (1×10−5, up to 15 epochs total), with batch normalisation kept in inference mode. Early stopping (patience 5) watches validation loss, and ReduceLROnPlateau cuts the rate by 0.2× after two stagnant epochs. Class weights inversely proportional to class frequency are applied in both phases.

Phase 1 — freeze base, train head (Adam 1e-3, 5 epochs)  →  Phase 2 — unfreeze top 30%, fine-tune (1e-5, early stopping, LR schedule)
Figure 3.2: The two-phase transfer-learning schedule used for every backbone.

3.1.5 Hybrid Ensemble Strategies

After training we build two ensemble heads on the validation split. In weighted soft voting, each model's softmax output is multiplied by that model's validation macro-F1 score and the weighted outputs are summed and normalised. In the stacked meta-classifier, the softmax outputs of all three models are concatenated into a 12-dimensional vector (4 classes × 3 models) and a logistic regression classifier (C = 1.0, up to 1000 iterations) is trained on these vectors using the validation split.

3.1.6 Grad-CAM Visualisation

To see which parts of a smear drove a prediction, we added Grad-CAM [6]. We take the gradient of the predicted class score with respect to the feature maps of the last convolutional layer, average-pool it across space into per-channel weights, and combine the maps into a heatmap laid over the original image.

3.1.7 MC Dropout Uncertainty Quantification (Novelty)

A softmax score on its own is a poor stand-in for confidence [16]. For a better estimate, we run MC Dropout at inference: for each image, every backbone makes 30 stochastic forward passes with its dropout layers left active. Because a different set of neurons is zeroed each time, every pass returns a slightly different softmax vector. From that spread we compute the predictive entropy (total uncertainty) and the mutual information (model, or epistemic, uncertainty). The predictive entropy is normalised by log(4) and mapped to a three-level confidence flag.

Table 3.2: Confidence flag thresholds.
Normalised EntropyFlagClinical Action
< 0.2HIGHAccept prediction
0.2 – 0.5MEDIUMReview recommended
> 0.5LOWRefer to specialist

Anything flagged LOW goes to a human specialist instead of being acted on directly. This selective referral step is the main contribution of the work.

3.2 System Requirements

3.2.1 Functional Requirements

  • Upload a blood smear image and return the predicted class with a confidence score.
  • Show per-backbone votes, a Grad-CAM overlay and an uncertainty flag for each prediction.
  • Run the data, training, ensemble and evaluation stages from the web interface.
  • Display evaluation metrics, confusion matrices and training curves.

3.2.2 Software Requirements

  • Python 3.10–3.12; TensorFlow/Keras, scikit-learn, NumPy, Pandas, Pillow, OpenCV.
  • FastAPI and Uvicorn for the backend; HTML/CSS/JavaScript with Chart.js for the front-end.
  • Operating system: Windows, Linux or macOS.

3.2.3 Hardware Requirements

  • Training: a GPU is recommended; the reported numbers were measured on CPU.
  • Inference: runs on CPU; 8 GB RAM or more is comfortable.

Chapter 5: Implementation and Testing

5.1 Technologies Used

We wrote the pipeline in Python 3.10. TensorFlow/Keras does the deep learning, scikit-learn handles the meta-classifier and the metrics, NumPy, Pandas and Pillow move the data around, and OpenCV blends the Grad-CAM overlays. The web application is built on FastAPI with a plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript front-end, and Chart.js draws the training curves and class distributions.

5.2 Procedures

5.2.1 Project Planning

The work was built in stages: scaffold, data, models and training, ensemble and prediction, evaluation, Grad-CAM, the web application and finally the documentation. Each stage had to run end-to-end in a fast "smoke" mode on a tiny sample before it was considered working.

5.2.2 Data Collection and Preparation

The dataset is downloaded from Kaggle with kagglehub, split with stratified sampling at seed 42, and cached to disk. When credentials are not available, a small synthetic demo set lets the whole pipeline run offline for testing.

5.2.3 Building the Pipeline

Each backbone is built with an ImageNet-pretrained base and a new head, fine-tuned in two phases, and saved as a .keras file. The ensemble weights and the stacked meta-classifier are fit on the validation split only. The better head by macro-F1 is persisted and used by the predictor.

5.2.4 Web Application

The FastAPI backend exposes endpoints for prediction, running each pipeline stage, and reading metrics. Long-running stages run on a background thread, and the browser follows their progress over Server-Sent Events. The front-end has seven views: Home, Preview, Performance Analysis, Chart, About and the Pipeline pages (Data, Train).

5.2.5 Debugging Real-Data Failures

Early runs surfaced the usual problems: class imbalance, batch-normalisation behaviour during fine-tuning, and Grad-CAM pointing at the wrong layer. These were fixed with class weights, by keeping batch-norm in inference mode in Phase 2, and by verifying the Grad-CAM target layer against the model's layer list.

5.3 Testing and Validation

5.3.1 Design of Test Cases

Automated tests use a tiny committed sample and build the backbones with random weights so they run without a network. They check the data splits, the model construction and Grad-CAM layer, the ensemble maths, the prediction output shape, the API error handling and the static front-end assets.

5.3.2 Validation

The whole pipeline is required to run end-to-end in smoke mode on CPU before any change is accepted. Every reported metric comes from an actual script run, with results/metrics.json as the single source of truth; the comparison table in the report is generated from it.

Chapter 7: Conclusion

We put together a hybrid ensemble for four-class ALL detection from blood smear images. Three pretrained CNN backbones were fine-tuned in two phases and then combined through weighted soft voting and a stacked meta-classifier, and the stacked ensemble reached 98.97% accuracy and a 0.9883 macro-F1. The piece we added, MC Dropout uncertainty, pulled confident predictions apart from shaky ones: it flagged 83% of the misclassifications while tripping on 5.6% of the correct ones (entropy AUROC 0.965; only 6 test errors, so these rates are indicative). Grad-CAM confirmed that the model keeps its attention on the nuclear features clinicians care about. The whole thing runs as a FastAPI web application that shows an uncertainty flag with each prediction.

The work has clear limits. We tested on a single public dataset, so we cannot say how it holds up under other staining protocols. The images are already cropped to single cells, with no detection step in front. And the dataset is small, at 3,242 images. These do not take away from the central result: an ensemble that is both accurate and honest about when it is unsure is more useful in a clinic than a slightly more accurate one that is not.

Chapter 8: Future Work

  • Test on blood smears from different hospitals and staining protocols to measure generalisation.
  • Add a cell-detection step in front so the system can work on whole-slide images instead of pre-cropped cells.
  • Fold a Vision Transformer into the ensemble alongside the CNN backbones.
  • Calibrate the confidence thresholds on a larger, more varied validation set.
  • Run a prospective study alongside pathologists to measure the real effect of selective referral.
